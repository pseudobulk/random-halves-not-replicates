# Random cell halves are not independent replicates in pooled perturbation screens

Xuanrui Huang

National University of Singapore, Singapore

Correspondence: huangxuanrui@u.nus.edu

<!-- Draft v2, 2026-10-18. -->

---

## Abstract

Single-cell perturbation screens are routinely checked by splitting each perturbation's cells into two random halves and asking whether the halves agree. This check sets the noise ceilings of model benchmarks, decides which perturbations count as reliable and separates reproducible from noisy effects. It assumes that the cells are independent replicates. In pooled screens they are not: the cells carrying one guide descend from a limited number of transduced clones, and in multiplexed drug screens many cell lines share a well. We compared random halves with halves that share no clone or well: different guides, donors or replicate plates. In a genome-scale screen of primary T cells, almost every perturbation that should do nothing looked reproducible under random halves, and only chance-level under independent guides. A clone marker shows why: both random halves contain the same few clones, and sequencing more cells does not help. Two vehicle-only wells of a drug screen likewise differed reproducibly. As a result, benchmark ceilings and reliability labels are inflated, and structure across cellular contexts can appear that independent replicates do not reproduce. The effect was large in a primary T-cell screen, smaller in a multiplexed drug screen and small in five cell-line screens and in the stem-cell data of the Virtual Cell Challenge. Random halves measure how precisely one set of clones or wells was sampled, not whether an experiment would replicate.

## Introduction

Large perturbation atlases now measure transcriptional responses to thousands of genetic and chemical perturbations across cell types, states and genetic backgrounds<sup>1–4</sup>. They are used to train and evaluate models that predict the effects of unseen perturbations or contexts<sup>5,6</sup>. How much of a measured response is reproducible, and therefore predictable at all, has become a central question, and recent work argues that benchmarks should report reproducibility ceilings alongside model scores<sup>7,8</sup>.

The usual way to estimate reproducibility is to split the cells of each perturbation at random into two halves and compare the two estimated responses. Three quantities in current use are computed in this way. The data ceiling of cell-eval, the evaluation tool of the Virtual Cell Challenge, compares disjoint random halves of each perturbation and of the controls and applies the Spearman–Brown correction to full depth<sup>5,9–11</sup>. Per-perturbation reliability labels, proposed to clean training and evaluation sets, are based on the agreement between random halves<sup>7</sup>. And decompositions of perturbation effects into parts shared across cellular contexts and parts specific to each context use random halves to separate reproducible from noisy variation<sup>12</sup>. Each of these assumes that the cells of a perturbation are independent samples, so that the two halves differ only by sampling noise.

In pooled screens this assumption is unlikely to hold. Lentiviral libraries are delivered at low multiplicity of infection, and the cells that carry a given guide at the time of sequencing descend from a limited number of transduced cells, often of unequal size<sup>13–15</sup>. In multiplexed drug screens, dozens of cell lines share each well, together with its drug exposure, density and handling<sup>4</sup>. Random halves of a perturbation's cells therefore share the state of the same clones or wells.

The statistical problem is familiar from single-cell differential expression, where treating cells from the same individual as independent inflates false discoveries<sup>16,17</sup>, and one recent study noted that random halves measure sampling precision rather than experimental reproducibility<sup>18</sup>. Clone barcodes have been used to follow clonal heterogeneity in phenotypic pooled screens<sup>13</sup>. To our knowledge, how large the effect is in transcriptional perturbation screens, where it comes from and what it does to the quantities above has not been measured.

We call a clone or well whose state is shared by a group of cells a unit, and halves of a perturbation's cells that share no unit independent units. We measured the effect in screens where independent units exist for the same perturbation and context (Fig. 1): the two guides that target each gene, which are independent transductions; different donors; replicate plates; and independent screens of the same cell line. As negative controls we used null perturbations, which have no expected effect. The results show that random halves can be dominated by shared clone or well states, that the size of this component differs between screens and donors, that it inflates benchmark ceilings and reliability labels, and that it carries cross-context structure that independent units do not reproduce.

## Results

### Null perturbations look reproducible under random halves

We first asked whether a perturbation with no expected effect looks reproducible when its cells are split at random. The genome-scale CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) screen of primary human CD4+ T cells<sup>3</sup> provides such perturbations: each gene is targeted by two guides, and some targets are measured by the probe panel but not expressed in T cells (control mean below 0.01 log1p CP10K, the logarithm of one plus counts per 10,000; 1,239 targets in donor 1 and 872 in donor 2). Knocking down a gene that is not expressed cannot change the transcriptome through that gene.

For each target we estimated its effect in two halves of its cells and computed the cross-half signal, the gene-wise product of the two estimates summed over genes. It estimates the energy, or sum of squares over genes, of the part of the effect that the two halves share. For a null perturbation whose halves differ only by sampling noise it is positive about half the time, which is what our pipeline returns for independent units in simulations (Supplementary Fig. S1a).

Under random halves, 96–99% of the unexpressed targets had a positive cross-half signal, in both donors and both states (Fig. 2a,b). With one guide in each half the share was 49–54%, and with donors 1–2 against donors 3–4 it was 53–55%, close to chance (Fig. 2b). Random halves therefore report a reproducible response for perturbations that should have none; independent units do not.

The same holds in a multiplexed drug screen. In Tahoe-100M, about 50 cancer cell lines share each well, and each plate includes vehicle-only (DMSO) wells<sup>4</sup>. The difference between two such wells has no biological reason to be nonzero, yet it was reproducible between random halves of the wells for every cell line on every plate (median between-half Pearson correlation 0.53; Fig. 2c), and most of it was common to all lines in the well (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Non-targeting guides, which have no target at all, behaved like unexpressed targets in the CD4 screen: 97–99% were positive under random halves, against 49–50% for halves taken from two different non-targeting guides (Fig. 2d; Supplementary Table S1). These are the control cells that benchmark tools also split at random.

### The shared state points to clones

In Tahoe the shared unit is the well. In the CD4 screen a clone marker identifies it as the transduced clone. The T-cell receptor (TCR) β rearrangement of a clone fixes its use of the two constant regions: rearrangements into the Jβ2 cluster delete TRBC1, whereas those into Jβ1 retain it. A clone therefore has a characteristic TRBC1 fraction that its cells share (Fig. 3a), and the probe panel measures both constant regions. If a perturbation's cells were a random sample of the population, the z-score of their mean TRBC1 fraction would have variance 1; it was 34 in donor 1 and 81 in donor 2 (Fig. 3b). The deviation was nearly identical between random halves (r = 0.93 and 0.96) and unrelated between guide halves (r ≈ 0; Fig. 3c): random halves contain the same clones, and the two guides contain different ones. The overdispersion implies that a perturbation's cells come from at most about 7 (donor 1) and 4 (donor 2) equally sized clones (Methods), although of the order of 500 cells per perturbation were transduced<sup>3</sup>. Expansion after transduction is very uneven.

The marker also tracks the expression signal. Among unexpressed targets, the more clonally skewed a perturbation's cells, the larger its random-half signal (Spearman correlation 0.21 and 0.24, TCR genes excluded; Fig. 3d), as expected for a one-bit marker of the clonal composition. Halves that keep the guides but separate the transductions, donors 1–2 against 3–4, remove most of the null signal, and pairing the same guide across donors adds little (57% and 54% positive, against 51% for different guides; Supplementary Table S2). The shared component is therefore a property of the transduced cells, not of the guide sequence.

In libraries selected on a growth phenotype, guide effects can mimic this pattern. Targets of the K562 essential-gene library<sup>1</sup> that are not expressed in K562 have reproducible effects that recur across two independent screens (85% of their within-screen structure; Supplementary Fig. S3), most likely through neighboring promoters<sup>19</sup>. Null controls from such libraries cannot separate clones from guide effects; independent transductions of the same guide can.

### Sampling more cells does not remove the shared component

If the shared component were sampling noise, more cells would shrink it. Splitting each perturbation's cells into quarters as well as halves, the null cross-half signal under random splits was the same at both depths, and under guide splits it was near zero at both (Fig. 3e). The between-half correlation of these null perturbations nevertheless rose with depth, from 0.14 to 0.24 in donor 1: with more cells, sampling noise falls while the shared component stays, so random-half reliability converges to the agreement within the same clones. The Spearman–Brown extrapolation predicts this rise correctly (0.25 against an observed 0.24); what it extrapolates is the agreement within one transduced population, not across experiments.

### How large the shared component is, and where

Most of what random halves call reproducible in the CD4 screen is shared only within one transduction. Under guide halves the reproducible energy of expressed targets fell by about 80% in donor 1 and 90% in donor 2. Pairing the same and different guides across donors separates the part of this loss that is due to the guide (Supplementary Table S2; Methods): guide-specific effects account for 8–15% of the random-half signal, and 66–81% is shared only by cells of one transduction (Fig. 3f). Donor 2, whose perturbations were more clonal by the TRBC marker, had the larger share.

Across screens, the size of the shared component follows the clonal structure. In five cell-line CRISPRi screens<sup>1,2</sup> and in the H1 embryonic stem cell data of the 2025 Virtual Cell Challenge<sup>5</sup>, non-targeting constructs were positive somewhat more often than chance under random halves (60–76%), but their random-half signal was 0.6–7% of that of a typical targeting perturbation; in the CD4 screen it exceeded it (1.2–1.3 times; Fig. 2d). In Tahoe, the reproducible energy within wells was 1.4–1.5 times that across replicate plates. The inflation is large where each perturbation is made up of few clones, as in primary cells, and small in the cell lines and in the H1 benchmark data. It also differs between donors of the same screen, so no single correction factor applies.

### Random-half ceilings and reliability labels are inflated

We ran cell-eval (version 0.8.2)<sup>9</sup>, the evaluation tool of the Virtual Cell Challenge, on 300 perturbations from resting cells of donor 1, half of them unexpressed targets and half strong knockdowns whose two guides were similarly effective, with equal numbers of cells per guide. We compared the tool's own random-half ceiling with the same metrics computed between the two guides of each perturbation.

The random-half ceiling exceeded the independent-guide ceiling on every metric (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Table S3). For the Pearson correlation of effects it was 0.32 against 0.06 over all 300 perturbations, and 0.38 against 0.13 for the strength-matched knockdowns, so the gap is not explained by one guide being weaker. Perturbations with no expected effect received high achievable scores, for example 0.26 for the Pearson correlation and 0.97 for discrimination between perturbations, while their independent-guide values stayed near the corrected chance level. For metrics whose chance level is 0.5, the Spearman–Brown correction itself maps chance to 0.67 (Fig. 4c), a separate problem that applying the correction to the chance-corrected score would avoid.

Reliability labels behave the same way. Under a recently proposed split-half rule<sup>7</sup>, 18% of unexpressed and 24% of expressed targets in donor 1 were labeled reliable with random halves, against 0.6% and 2.2% with guide halves (Fig. 4b); in donor 2, whose perturbations were more clonal, the random-half shares were 42% and 52%. In Tahoe, ceilings from random halves within wells exceeded those between the replicate plates (Pearson correlation 0.77–0.87 against 0.64; Fig. 4d).

### Shared units carry cross-context structure that independent units do not reproduce

Perturbation effects measured in several contexts are often decomposed into a response shared across contexts and context-specific responses, with random halves used to correct for noise<sup>12</sup>. Such decompositions underlie models that transfer perturbation effects to unseen cellular contexts<sup>12</sup>, a task that current benchmarks increasingly target<sup>5</sup>. When contexts share units, a unit's deviation enters both parts (Fig. 5a). In Tahoe, every line in a well experiences that well's deviation. In the CD4 screen, resting and stimulated cells were split from one culture after expansion<sup>3</sup>, and a perturbation's TRBC1 deviation correlated between the two states nearly as strongly as between random halves (r = 0.89 and 0.94).

The share of reproducible energy that is context-specific was stable across designs, about 0.3 in CD4 and 0.36–0.39 in Tahoe, but the energies behind it were not: in CD4 both parts were 8–9 times larger under random halves than under guide halves (Fig. 5b). A stable share therefore does not show that an estimate is free of shared-unit effects.

A linear predictor of the context-specific responses from the shared response, scored on held-out perturbations, worked within units and failed across them. In Tahoe, with the target fixed, the shared response from the same wells explained 0.30–0.33 of the target's reproducible energy, and that from the replicate plate 0.04–0.09 (Fig. 5c). In CD4 it explained about 0.5 under random halves and about 0 under guide halves, including strong knockdowns with guides of equal strength and the same guide in independent donors (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Table S2). Under random halves it passed on the structure of unexpressed targets at the same rate as that of expressed targets (Supplementary Table S4): it reads the state of the units, not the response to the perturbation. For K562 and RPE1 cells screened separately<sup>1</sup>, replacing the K562 part of the shared response by an independent screen left the predictor unchanged (Fig. 5d).

A three-term model reproduces these patterns (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. S1; Methods). Each cell carries an additive state of its unit, a multiplicative gain of its unit on the perturbation's response, and sampling noise. The additive term makes null perturbations reproducible and inflates ceilings; neither unit term shrinks with more cells; and when contexts share units, a predictor appears where none exists (explained share 0.10 with a true value of 0). The model also shows the reverse risk: large unit deviations along the response directions can hide a real cross-context structure across independent units. At the unit-to-signal energy ratios of the CD4 screen (4.0 and 7.5; Methods), a true explained share of 0.70 is reduced to 0.10 and 0.03, so the absence of a predictor across guides shows only that it is not usable across independent units.

### When can random halves be trusted?

These results suggest a simple check (Box 1).

<!-- BOX START -->
**Box 1 | Checking whether random halves can be trusted**

*Do the two halves share clones or wells?* Halves formed by different guides, donors or replicate plates do not. They can be used as replicates, and a null control should still be reported.

*If they do* (random halves of the same cells, or cells from the same well), reliability, ceilings and cross-context structure will be inflated. The inflation is likely to be large in primary cells, after strong bottlenecks, with several cell lines per well and when the same clones are measured in several conditions. It was small in five cell-line screens and in the H1 data of the 2025 Virtual Cell Challenge, where the random-half signal of non-targeting constructs was 0.6–7% of that of a typical targeting perturbation.

*In every case*, include perturbations that should do nothing, such as non-targeting guides or unexpressed targets. Reference them to their own mean, not to the mean of all perturbations, and do not take them from libraries selected on a growth phenotype.

## Discussion

Random halves of a perturbation's cells answer a narrower question than the one they are usually asked. They estimate how precisely the response of a given set of clones, in a given well, has been sampled, not how well an independent experiment would reproduce it. Random halves remain the right tool when the question is sampling precision within one experiment, for example whether more cells of the same population would sharpen an estimate; they are the wrong tool when the question is what an independent experiment would show. Independent transductions of the same construct answer that question, and different guides answer a third one, how reproducible the effect of the gene is, for which differences between guides are part of the answer. The difference is not a small correction. In a primary T-cell screen, most of what random halves call reproducible was shared only within one transduction, most likely by clones; perturbations with no expected effect received high ceilings and reliability labels; and in a multiplexed drug screen, the well contributed a stable deviation that was mostly shared by the lines in it.

The statistical problem is known from single-cell differential expression<sup>16,17</sup>, and that random halves measure sampling precision has been noted before<sup>18</sup>. Our results add three points. First, we compared random halves with independent units within the same data, together with null perturbations and a direct clone marker; this points to clones in the T-cell screen and to wells in the drug screen, and measures the size of the shared component. Second, the component does not shrink when more cells of the same population are sequenced, so this remedy, implied by sampling models of reliability<sup>7</sup>, does not remove it; with more cells, random-half reliability converges to the agreement within clones. Third, shared units affect not only reproducibility but also structure across contexts. When the same well or clone is present in several contexts, its deviation enters the shared and the context-specific responses together, and a predictor that links them can hold within the same units and fail across independent units. This matters for models that transfer perturbation effects across cellular contexts, a task that current benchmarks increasingly target<sup>5</sup>.

The original studies used appropriate replicate units for their own analyses: the CD4 study modeled donors as biological replicates<sup>3</sup>, and the Tahoe-100M study included a replicate plate and reported cross-plate concordance<sup>4</sup>. The issue arises when random halves are used downstream, in benchmark ceilings, reliability labels, denoising and cross-context modeling. A concurrent study found with replicate plates that the context-specific part of drug responses in Tahoe-100M is weakly reproducible<sup>8</sup>, consistent with our cross-plate results. In the H1 data of the 2025 Virtual Cell Challenge the shared component was small (Fig. 2d), so ceilings computed on those data are little affected; the inflation matters most where each perturbation is made up of few clones, as in primary cells.

Our analysis has limitations. Tahoe-100M contains one full pair of replicate plates (6 and 14, sharing 94 conditions; no other pair of plates shares more than 13), and we analyzed only this pair. The DMSO well difference had a component that recurred across plates (median cross-plate correlation 0.35 for the same line), possibly reflecting well position, so cross-plate comparisons may still be somewhat optimistic. The same-data comparisons in CD4 used two donors. Guide halves differ in the guide as well as in clones. We estimated the guide-specific part by pairing the same guide across donors and matched knockdown strength, but that comparison crosses donors and uses pseudobulk; independent transductions of the same guide within one donor would be a cleaner unit. Targets outside the probe panel were excluded from the null set. Finally, the model reproduces the observed patterns but was not fitted to the data.

Future screens can build in independent units. Two or more guides per gene are already standard, and clone barcodes add little cost<sup>13</sup>; separate transductions, donors and replicate plates with randomized layouts cost more but give the most direct test. For existing data, a set of null perturbations is the minimum check.

## Methods

### Data

**Primary human CD4+ T cells.** The genome-scale CRISPRi Perturb-seq screen of Zhu et al.<sup>3</sup> uses a probe-based readout in cells from four donors, in resting and stimulated (48 h) states, with two guides per target. We used the single-cell files of donors 1 and 2 in both states, after removing cells flagged as low quality or without an assigned guide, and the released pseudobulk counts of all four donors.

**Tahoe-100M.** In Tahoe-100M<sup>4</sup>, about 50 cancer cell lines are pooled in each well of 14 plates; plate 14 is a biological replicate of plate 6. We streamed all cells, normalized each cell to 10,000 counts over all genes, applied log1p and averaged by cell line, sample and half. Each cell's half was the parity of a CRC32 hash of its identifier, which acts as a random bit per cell and does not depend on the well, sequencing run or quality of the cell. We applied no filtering beyond that of the released data.

**Replogle et al.** We used the raw single cells of the K562 essential-gene screen (day 6), part 1 of the K562 genome-wide screen (day 8) and the RPE1 essential-gene screen<sup>1</sup>. Counts were normalized to 10,000 over the genes common to the three screens and log1p transformed, with an additive correction of the control mean within each GEM group (one 10x Genomics channel).

**Nadig et al.** The raw single cells of the Jurkat and HepG2 essential-gene screens<sup>2</sup> were used in Supplementary Table S4 and for the non-targeting analysis (Fig. 2d).

**Virtual Cell Challenge 2025.** The H1 hESC training data<sup>5</sup> (221,273 cells, 150 targets and 31 non-targeting constructs, 10x Flex) were used only for the non-targeting analysis (Fig. 2d).

### Effects and gene filters

The effect of a perturbation in a set of its cells is the mean log1p CP10K expression of those cells minus the mean of control cells, which were non-targeting guides (CD4 and Replogle) or DMSO wells (Tahoe). In CD4 and Tahoe, control cells were split in the same way as the perturbed cells; in the Replogle data, both halves were referenced to all control cells, which cancels after centering. The target gene was set to zero in its own effect, except in the ceiling analyses and the template check, which keep it as the benchmark tools do. Unless stated otherwise, effects were centered over perturbations within each half and context.

Genes were kept according to their control mean log1p CP10K. In CD4 single cells the threshold was 0.1 in both states for analyses that combine states, and in the analyzed state otherwise; in the CD4 donor pseudobulk it was 0.1 in every donor and state; in Tahoe, 0.1 in the plate-6 DMSO cells of all lines; and in the Replogle data, 0.1 in all three screens, with the 2,000 genes of highest control mean used for the template check. For cell-eval, genes with control mean of at least 0.01 in resting cells were kept, together with the target genes. TCR genes (TRAC, TRBC, TRAV, TRBV, TRGC and TRDC) were excluded from the CD4 null-share, depth, clonal-energy and TRBC-link analyses.

### Splits

In CD4, each cell carries two independent random bits, r1 and r2, drawn with a fixed seed (21). Random halves are r1 = 0 against r1 = 1; for the bootstrap and the reliability labels, two further halvings used r2 and r1 ⊕ r2. Random quarters are (r1 = 0, r2 = 0) against (r1 = 1, r2 = 0). Guide halves are defined by the parity of the guide number in the guide name (for example "-1" against "-2"), so each guide falls on the same side in every file, and control cells were split in the same way by non-targeting guide; guide quarters are (parity 0, r2 = 0) against (parity 1, r2 = 0). Donor halves, of which there is a single pairing of donor groups, sum the pseudobulk counts over donors 1–2 or 3–4, over both guides and both sequencing runs; their effects are log1p CP10K of the summed counts minus that of the summed controls, with at least 40 cells per half and state. In the Replogle data, the cells of each perturbation were permuted (seeds 0, 1 and 2 for the three screens) and cut into two equal halves, dropping one cell when the number was odd. In Tahoe, halves were the hashed cell bit within each well, and independent units were the two replicate plates.

### Unexpressed and expressed targets

Unexpressed targets are targets measured by the probe panel with control mean log1p CP10K below 0.01; expressed targets have a control mean of at least 0.5. Unexpressed targets are an operational null: their knockdown cannot act through the target gene, but a guide can still act elsewhere, for example at a neighboring promoter. Analyses that combine the two states (Fig. 2a,b, the context-specific share, the predictor and the reliability labels) apply the threshold in both states. Analyses within one state (depth, clonal energy and ceilings) apply it in that state, since a gene that is silent in one state cannot act there. In the Replogle data, which release only expressed genes, targets absent from the gene list were treated as unexpressed. The K562 analysis across screens used the 178 targets that were unexpressed in both K562 screens.

In CD4, perturbations entered Fig. 2a,b if they had at least 10 cells on each side of both the random and the guide halves in both states (1,239 unexpressed targets in donor 1 and 872 in donor 2), the depth and clone tests if they had at least 8 cells on each side of every quarter design, and the predictor, context-specific share and reliability labels if they had at least 10 cells on each side of all four halvings (three random and one guide) in both states.

### Non-targeting guides

Each non-targeting guide of CD4 donors 1 and 2, resting and stimulated, was treated as a perturbation (a separate streaming pass over the single cells, keeping non-targeting cells only, with the same random bit per cell as the other analyses). Its effect in a half is its mean log1p CP10K minus the mean over all kept non-targeting guides in that half. Genes had a mean of at least 0.1 over all non-targeting cells, TCR genes excluded, and guides needed at least 10 cells in each half. Random halves split each guide's cells by the random bit. For halves from different guides, guides were paired at random and the first half of one guide was compared with the second half of the other, so that both designs have the same depth; the share of positive pairs is the mean over 50 random pairings. The TRBC clone marker was computed as for targeting guides, over non-targeting guides with at least 30 cells carrying TRBC counts. The same analysis was run on the raw single cells of the Replogle K562 essential-gene (day 6), K562 genome-wide (day 8, part 1) and RPE1 screens<sup>1</sup> the Nadig Jurkat and HepG2 screens<sup>2</sup> and the H1 data of the 2025 Virtual Cell Challenge<sup>5</sup>, with one pseudo-perturbation per non-targeting construct (sgID_AB, or guide_id in the H1 data). Targeting perturbations were grouped by target gene, referenced to all non-targeting cells of the half and centred over perturbations; the ratio in Fig. 2d is the mean random-half cross-half signal of non-targeting constructs divided by that of targeting perturbations, with the same genes (mean of at least 0.1 over non-targeting cells) and at least 10 cells per half. In CD4 the same ratio was computed on resting cells, with TCR genes excluded.

### Cross-half signal and null perturbations

The cross-half signal of a perturbation is Σ_g x_A,g x_B,g, the gene-wise product of its two half-effects summed over genes. With independent noise in the two halves, it is an unbiased estimate of the energy of the reproducible effect. For null perturbations, cross-half signals were referenced to the null baseline: the mean effect of the unexpressed targets in each half was subtracted from every perturbation. Referencing instead to the mean of all perturbations (template removal) makes a null perturbation equal to the negative template in both halves. We tested this in the K562 genome-wide screen, with halves from a random bit per cell (seed 9) and at least 15 cells per half; the share of reproducible null targets rose from 83% to 89%.

In CD4, the share of the random-half signal of expressed targets that the other guide does not reproduce is 1 minus the ratio of the guide-half to the random-half cross-half signal, in the same state and for the same targets. To split this share into guide-specific and transduction-level parts, we used the released pseudobulk of all four donors. For each target we formed four pseudobulk effects per state: guide rank 0 or 1 (the guide's rank within its target) in donors 1–2 or 3–4, each the log1p CP10K of the summed counts of both sequencing runs minus that of the summed non-targeting controls of the same donors and state, with at least 20 cells each. The same-guide design pairs guide 0 in donors 1–2 with guide 0 in donors 3–4, and likewise for guide 1; the different-guide design pairs guide 0 in donors 1–2 with guide 1 in donors 3–4, and vice versa. Both designs use the same cells, and genes, unexpressed and expressed targets follow the donor-half analysis. The difference between the two designs, divided by the different-guide signal, estimates guide-specific energy relative to the signal shared across guides (0.80 resting, 0.48 stimulated). Multiplying it by the single-cell guide-half signal gives the guide-specific part of the random-half signal, and the remainder above the guide-half signal is the transduction-level part. The single-cell guide-half signal also contains donor-specific responses, which the across-donor pseudobulk excludes, so this transfer tends to overestimate the guide-specific part. The predictor was run on both designs with the second implementation. For K562, we first computed the share of the unexpressed-target structure that recurs across the two independent K562 screens as the summed cross-screen signal divided by the geometric mean of the summed within-screen signals of the day-6 and day-8 screens, which gave 0.85. The unit-to-signal energy ratio used in the text is the null cross-half signal under random halves divided by the expressed cross-half signal under guide halves, the same definition as in the simulation.

### TRBC clone marker

We used each cell with at least two TRBC1 plus TRBC2 counts (1.75 million resting and 1.58 million stimulated cells of donor 1) and computed its TRBC1 fraction f = TRBC1 / (TRBC1 + TRBC2). For a perturbation with n such cells, z = (mean f − f0) / (sd_f / √n), where f0 and sd_f are the mean and standard deviation over all cells; under random assignment, var(z) ≈ 1. The variance of z was computed over perturbations with at least 30 cells, and the permutation value is the mean over five shuffles of perturbation labels. Reproducibility of the deviation (mean f − f0) is its Pearson correlation between halves across perturbations with at least 15 cells per half. Clonal skew is |z|. Its link to the cross-half expression signal (Fig. 3d) used the unexpressed targets of Fig. 2a,b with at least 30 cells carrying TRBC counts (1,234 in donor 1 and 864 in donor 2), with quartiles taken over these targets. A separate run with the per-state definition of unexpressed targets gave Spearman correlations of 0.229 and 0.227 (resting) and 0.159 and 0.160 (stimulated) in donors 1 and 2.


For the effective number of clones, a perturbation whose n cells come from K equally sized clones with intra-clone correlation ICC of the TRBC1 fraction has E[z²] ≈ 1 + ICC(n/K − 1), so K ≤ n/z² for ICC ≤ 1. We report the median of n/z² over perturbations with z² > 1 (85–91% of them) in resting cells, with all guides of a perturbation pooled. The original design transduced 30 million cells per donor at a multiplicity of infection of about 0.2 with 23,256 guides<sup>3</sup>, of the order of 250 transduced cells per guide and 500 per perturbation. For the comparison of states, the TRBC1 deviation of each perturbation with at least 30 cells carrying TRBC counts in both resting and stimulated cells was correlated between the two states (9,830 perturbations in donor 1 and 9,037 in donor 2).
### Depth

The depth test compared the half and quarter designs on the same targets. In the model below, the unit term scales with 1/K_eff, the inverse effective number of clones of a perturbation, and not with the number of cells sampled. The between-half Pearson correlation is the mean over targets of the per-target correlation between half-effects, and the Spearman–Brown value 2r/(1 + r) was applied to that mean.

### Shared and context-specific responses

For two contexts X and Y, the shared response is (X + Y)/2 and the context-specific response is (X − Y)/2, after centering each context over perturbations. For Tahoe, with M the line × condition matrix of effects for one gene, the context-specific part is γ = M − line mean − condition mean + grand mean and the shared part is β = condition mean − grand mean. Conditions were the (drug, concentration) pairs present on both plates. Lines were kept if they had at least 10 cells per half on both plates in at least 85% of conditions, and conditions were kept if every kept line qualified, leaving 48 lines and 91 conditions. The context-specific share is the cross-half signal of the context-specific part divided by the sum of the shared and context-specific cross-half signals; for Tahoe the shared energy is multiplied by the number of lines, so that both parts are summed over line × condition entries. The within-well energy is the cross-half signal between the two halves of one plate, the cross-plate energy is that between plates 6 and 14, and the signal correlation is the cross-plate energy divided by the geometric mean of the two within-well energies.

### Shared-to-specific predictor

In the main implementation, used for CD4 donors 1 and 2 and for Tahoe, the features of a perturbation are its shared response in half A projected on the top k right singular vectors of the shared responses of the training perturbations (k = 20 for CD4 and 10 for Tahoe). A ridge regression maps the features to the context-specific response in half B, with λ chosen from {10⁻³, 10⁻², …, 10²} × tr(ZᵀZ)/k by five-fold inner cross-validation to maximize the held-out explained energy. Perturbations (in Tahoe, conditions) were split into five outer folds. The explained share is (Σ‖γ_B‖² − Σ‖γ_B − F‖²) / Σ γ_A · γ_B, where F is the held-out prediction and the denominator is the reproducible energy of the target. A shuffled control, which permutes the features across perturbations before cross-validation, gave values near zero throughout. Centering over perturbations was done before cross-validation and in the same way for every design; principal directions and λ were estimated within the training folds. In Tahoe the regression was fitted separately for each line; the target was half 1 of one plate, and the shared response came either from half 0 of the same wells or from the other plate with both halves pooled.

A second implementation, used for the CD4 robustness checks and strength-matched subsets and for K562–RPE1, uses two outer and two inner folds, λ up to 10³ and k = 10 and 20 (5–50 in the robustness checks; Supplementary Table S5). For K562–RPE1, perturbations needed at least 20 cells in all three screens, and p53-pathway genes, cell-cycle genes and TP53 itself were excluded as perturbations; the independent shared response replaced the K562 half by the full other K562 screen, (other K562 screen + RPE1 half A)/2. Strength-matched knockdowns in CD4 are expressed targets (at least 0.5 in both states) whose two guides both knocked down the target, with the weaker guide reaching a Δlog1p of at most −0.25 and at least 70% of the stronger guide, in both states; effects were re-centered within the subset, which held 615 targets in donor 1 and 470 in donor 2. The text and Fig. 5d give k = 20; with k = 10 the explained shares were 0.20 and 0.29.

The two implementations differ in folds, ridge solver and filters, and gave the same conclusions: in CD4 donor 1, 0.446 and 0.469 under random halves and at most 0.004 under guide halves; in Tahoe, 0.23–0.32 and 0.30–0.33 for the same wells and 0.02–0.07 and 0.04–0.09 for the replicate plate.

### Ceilings

We installed cell-eval version 0.8.2 (PyPI, 27 July 2026)<sup>9</sup> in an isolated environment. Perturbations were taken from resting cells of donor 1, with at least 40 cells per guide: 150 unexpressed targets drawn at random, and the 150 strong knockdowns with the largest mean knockdown among targets that met the strength-matching rule above in resting cells. Each perturbation was capped at 400 cells and 15,000 control cells were drawn; cells were then downsampled to equal numbers per guide (median 62 per side), and perturbations with fewer than 10 cells per side were dropped. Both designs used the same cells. The random-half ceiling is the tool's `compute_ceiling` with seeds 0–2. The guide ceiling runs the tool's `compute` with one guide's cells as "real" and the other's as "prediction", in both directions, and applies the tool's Spearman–Brown function to the mean over perturbations. Our aggregation of the per-perturbation results reproduced the tool's own aggregate exactly, and results were summarized over perturbations present in all runs. For Tahoe, Pearson and L1 discrimination ceilings were computed with the same definitions on pseudobulk means; across plates, half 0 of one plate was compared with half 1 of the other.

### Reliability labels

Following ref. 7, ρ is the Spearman–Brown value of the median between-half Pearson correlation, and perturbations with ρ ≥ 0.5 are labeled reliable. For random halves the median was taken over the three halvings r1, r2 and r1 ⊕ r2, which share cells and are therefore not independent resamples; guide halves have a single split. Undefined values were counted as not reliable. Resting cells of donor 1 were used.

### Tahoe DMSO wells

For each plate and cell line with at least 20 cells per half in both of the first two DMSO wells, the well difference in half h is x_h = well 1 (half h) − well 2 (half h). Its cross-half signal and between-half Pearson correlation were computed over genes, for plates with at least five such lines (47–48 lines on every plate). The size of the well effect is half the median cross-half signal of the well difference, since a difference carries two wells, divided by the median within-well cross-half signal of drug effects (200 random drug samples per plate, referenced to the mean of the two DMSO wells); the median over plates is reported. The line-shared fraction is n_lines (m_A · m_B) / [n_lines (m_A · m_B) + Σ_lines (d_A · d_B)], where m is the mean well difference over lines and d is each line's deviation from that mean.

### Model and simulation

A cell of perturbation p in context c from unit k has expression μ_pc(1 + g_k) + a_k + e, with unit state a_k ~ N(0, σ_a²I), unit gain g_k ~ N(0, σ_g²) and noise e ~ N(0, σ_e²I). For random halves, E[x_A · x_B] = |μ|²(1 + σ_g²/K_eff) + Gσ_a²/K_eff, with K_eff = 1/Σ_k w_k², where w_k are the unit weights; for independent halves it is |μ|². The simulation used G = 1,000 genes, 800 perturbations (20% null) and true responses of rank 10, with effect sizes s_p drawn from a log-normal distribution (log-mean log 0.3, s.d. 0.5) and context-specific part s_p(0.5 z_p R W₂ + 0.3 d_p). Each perturbation and transduction has K = 6 units with Dirichlet(0.5) weights, σ_a = 0.2, σ_g = 0.6 and σ_e = 1, and each half draws 60 cells across units from a multinomial distribution. Units are either shared between contexts, with the additive part scaled by ρ = 0.5 in the second context, or separate. In the fabrication block, the effect scale was 0.1, there was no true shared-to-specific structure and additive unit states lay along the shared response directions. In the attenuation block, the true explained share was about 0.70 and σ_a was 0.2, 0.6, 1.2, 1.5, 2.0 or 2.5, either isotropic or along the shared response directions; σ_a of 1.5 and 2.0 give unit-to-signal energy ratios of 4.1–4.2 and 7.2–7.6, the range of the CD4 screen. The predictor was a two-fold version of the one above (k = 10, λ from 10⁻² to 10³), and results are means over three seeds.

### Statistics

Confidence intervals are within-experiment intervals: 2.5th–97.5th bootstrap percentiles over 1,000 resamples of perturbations (in Tahoe, conditions), with predictions held fixed; Supplementary Table S4 used 500 resamples. These intervals describe variation over perturbations within one experiment. The independent units available were two donors in the CD4 single-cell data, four donors in its pseudobulk, two K562 screens and one pair of Tahoe plates; agreement across these units is reported separately rather than folded into the intervals. In CD4, the three random halvings, which share cells and are therefore not independent resamples, gave nearly identical estimates (for example, an explained share of 0.468–0.471 in donor 1). Shares of positive null perturbations from separate extractions and halvings, with different random halves and minimum-cell filters, agreed within 1.5 percentage points (for example, 98.4–99.0% in resting and 96.2–97.7% in stimulated cells of donor 1). Figures and text use the per-cell extraction, a single pass over the cells that records each cell's target, guide parity, two random bits and TRBC counts. Expectations for donor 2 were written and committed in two sets, each before the corresponding analysis, and all were met; one value came close to its limit (Supplementary Table S6).
